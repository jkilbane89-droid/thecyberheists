How the Discord leaks happened: the airman who read what he didn't need

Published 2026-09-29 · 5 min read · Missing control: Need-to-know access with monitoring

One of the worst U.S. intelligence leaks in years came from an IT specialist whose job was keeping military networks running, not reading what was on them, and his supervisors had caught him reading anyway.[1][3] This case file covers how hundreds of classified pages ended up in a gaming chat, how the warning signs were handled, what it cost, and the control that was missing.

What happened

Jack Teixeira enlisted in the Massachusetts Air National Guard in September 2019 and served with the 102nd Intelligence Wing at Otis Air National Guard Base as a cyber transport systems specialist, which meant looking after communications networks.[1][3] In 2021 he received a Top Secret clearance with access to especially sensitive compartmented information.[1]

From around January 2022, prosecutors said, he shared classified information in a private group on Discord, a chat platform popular with gamers. Over time that included photos of documents with SECRET and TOP SECRET markings, covering material such as Russia's war in Ukraine and troop movements.[1][4]

In the fall of 2022, according to the Air Force Inspector General, he was caught writing classified information on sticky notes, and the incident was not reported to security. Prosecutors said his superiors warned him twice against taking notes on classified intelligence and digging into material that had nothing to do with his job.[1][3]

The documents spread from the chat to other social media sites and eventually to pro-Russian bloggers.[2] The FBI arrested him at his home in North Dighton on April 13, 2023, when he was 21.[4]

How it worked

Nothing was hacked. Teixeira used access he legitimately held. His clearance let him into classified systems and facilities, and his IT role put him around those systems every day.[1][3]

The gap was between what he could see and what his job needed. The Inspector General found that unit policy gave technical staff more exposure to classified material than their duties required, and that he could spend time in secure areas without close supervision.[3] That left room to search intelligence reporting well beyond his duties, which is exactly what his superiors had warned him about.[1]

The bigger failure was what happened after people noticed. The Inspector General found that unit members knew of his intelligence-seeking behavior, recognized it as a warning sign of an insider threat, and did not pass the full details to security officials.[3]

How it was caught

He was not stopped by his own organization's alarms. Once the documents spread publicly, investigators traced them back through the chat group. Discord's account records listed his name and home address, and another user of the group identified him. Government monitoring also showed he had searched classified reporting for the word leak.[4]

What it cost

Teixeira pleaded guilty in March 2024 to 6 counts of willful retention and transmission of national defense information. On November 12, 2024, a federal judge in Boston sentenced him to 15 years in prison, followed by 3 years of supervised release.[1][2] Attorney General Merrick Garland said he had shared secrets to impress anonymous online friends.[1]

In December 2023 the Air Force disciplined 15 people. Actions ranged from removal from command to non-judicial punishment, and the 102nd Intelligence Wing's intelligence mission was moved to other units.[3]

The missing control

The missing control: need-to-know access, backed by monitoring of searches outside a person's job, with a clear rule that red flags go to security the day they are seen.

A clearance says someone can be trusted with secrets. Need-to-know says which secrets. If an IT specialist's access had been limited to what his network duties required, most of what he leaked would have been out of reach. If searches outside his role had triggered a review, and if the supervisors who saw him taking notes had reported it rather than handling it quietly, the leak could have been stopped months earlier.[3]

What to do in your business

  • Match access to the job. For each role, write down which folders, systems and customer records it actually needs, and remove everything else. Your IT person needs to manage the file server, not read payroll.
  • Turn on access logging for sensitive data. Most cloud file and accounting tools can log who opened what. Review the log monthly for people opening files outside their work.
  • Make reporting the default. Tell managers in writing that any concern about someone mishandling data must be reported to the owner or security lead, even if they have already spoken to the person.
  • Watch for unusual copying. Flag bulk downloads, printing of sensitive reports and uploads to personal cloud or chat accounts.
  • Recheck access after warnings. When someone is warned about data handling, review and reduce their access the same day.
