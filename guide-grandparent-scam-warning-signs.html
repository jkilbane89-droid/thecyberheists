Grandparent scam warning signs: what to do when a "grandchild" calls in trouble

Published 2026-11-11 · 6 min read

The phone rings and a young voice says, "Grandma, it's me. I'm in trouble." There's been a car accident, or an arrest, and they need money right now. Please don't tell Mom and Dad. This is the grandparent scam, also called the family emergency scam. It works because it turns love and panic into speed. This guide explains how the scam runs, a real case that ended in federal prison sentences, the warning signs to share with older relatives, and what to do if money has already changed hands.

How the scam works

It usually starts with a call from someone pretending to be a grandchild or other relative. The voice may sound a little off, and the caller has a ready excuse: a broken nose from the crash, a bad line, a cold. They need bail, a lawyer or a hospital payment, and they beg you to keep it secret. Then a second person often comes on the line pretending to be a lawyer, a police officer or a court official, to make the story feel official.[1]

The payment step is what gives it away. Scammers ask for gift cards, a wire transfer, cryptocurrency or a stack of cash. In a bolder version, they send a "courier" to your door to collect the cash in person.[1] Once you pay, the calls often keep coming: another fee, another fine, another emergency.[2]

Some scammers now use artificial intelligence to copy a relative's voice from short clips posted on social media, which makes the call sound far more convincing.[2] The voice is no longer proof. The story and the payment request are what to judge.

A real case: call centers, couriers and $3 million

From August 2020 to May 2021, callers working from call centers in Canada phoned older Americans and told them a grandchild or other relative had an emergency, usually a car accident, and urgently needed money. People posing as "couriers" then picked up the cash at the victims' homes. Others laundered the money through banks and cryptocurrency exchanges. Hundreds of victims across the United States, including in Kentucky, lost more than $3 million.[3]

Six people were convicted in federal court in the Western District of Kentucky. Christopher Courcoulacos, a Canadian citizen the court treated as a manager or supervisor, was sentenced to 6 years in prison. Mark Anthony Phillips of Ruskin, Florida, who pleaded guilty to money laundering conspiracy, also received 6 years. Couriers received sentences from 6 months to 18 months.[3]

The last defendant, Phillipe Gravel-Nadon, another manager in the scheme, was extradited from Colombia. In June 2025 he was sentenced to 5 years and 1 month in federal prison and ordered to pay $963,290 in restitution. The U.S. Attorney's advice afterward was simple: be skeptical of any call that presents an urgent situation, and hang up when the caller pushes harder for money.[3]

The warning signs

If a call has any of these, slow down. Do not pay yet:

  • A relative is suddenly in an emergency, such as an accident, an arrest or trouble while traveling abroad.[2]
  • "Don't tell anyone." A request for secrecy, especially from the grandchild's parents, is the biggest red flag.[1]
  • The voice sounds different and the caller has an excuse for it.[1]
  • A "lawyer," "officer" or "court clerk" joins the call, or a rapid series of calls keeps the pressure on.[2]
  • You are told to act right now or things will get worse.[2]
  • You are asked to pay with gift cards, a wire, cryptocurrency, or cash, whether mailed or handed to a courier. The FTC says anyone who asks for gift cards or a wire transfer is a scammer.[1]
  • Someone offers to come to your home to pick up the money.[1]

What to do during the call

  • Hang up. You are allowed to end the call, even if the caller is crying.
  • Call the grandchild back on a number you already have. If you can't reach them, call their parent or another relative, even though the caller told you not to.[1]
  • Ask for the family code word, if you have one. A real relative will know it. A scammer, even one using a cloned voice, won't.[2]
  • Check any official yourself. Look up the jail, court or police department on your own and call that number. Police do not call relatives to collect bail for someone else.[2]
  • Never give out your address or agree to a pickup.[1]

If you already paid

  • Call your bank or card company right away using the number on your card. Ask them to stop or reverse the payment.[2]
  • Sent a wire? Call the money transfer company's fraud line as soon as possible.[2]
  • Paid with gift cards? Call the company that issued the cards, tell them the cards were used in a scam, and keep the cards and receipts.
  • Report it to your local police, at ReportFraud.ftc.gov and at ic3.gov.[1][2] People 60 and older can call the National Elder Fraud Hotline at 1-833-372-8311.
  • Expect more calls. Scammers often call back for more money or offer to "recover" what you lost for a fee.[1] Hang up on both.
  • Don't be ashamed. These are organized operations with scripts and managers. Telling family quickly gives the bank the best chance to act.

Simple rules for families

  • Pick a family code word today, one that isn't posted anywhere online, and make sure grandparents know it.[2]
  • Make one rule: nobody in the family ever sends money for an emergency without first calling someone else in the family. Real emergencies survive a five-minute phone call.
  • Share less in public. Travel plans and family details posted publicly give scammers their script.[1]
  • Write the real numbers down next to the phone: each grandchild, their parents, and the bank.

Common questions

It really sounded like my grandson. Could it still be a scam?

Yes. A bad line and an excuse can cover a stranger's voice, and AI tools can copy a real voice from short clips online.[2] Call back on a number you know before doing anything.

The caller said he was a lawyer and gave me a case number. Is that real?

Anyone can make up a name and a case number. Look up the court or jail yourself and call it directly. A real lawyer or court will never ask for gift cards or send a courier for cash.
